Ridar: turning the prototype into a working product

A build specification for the commercial awareness globe — where the news actually comes from, what the database looks like, the prompts that write the summaries, and the order to build it in.

Handoff document · v1 · companion to index.html

The prototype is complete as an interface: a rotatable globe with ten briefed markets, six cross-cutting themes, three explanation levels, a daily quiz and accounts with streaks. Everything it shows is sample text held in a JavaScript object. This document covers what has to be built behind it.

There are three moving parts, and only one of them is hard.

  1. Getting the news in. Legally and reliably. This is the hard part, and it is a licensing problem more than an engineering one.
  2. Turning it into three-level summaries and quiz questions. One scheduled job, one AI model, one database write.
  3. Serving it to the front end. The prototype already has the shape; it needs to read from an API instead of a constant.

Section 01The news problem

You cannot take Financial Times or Bloomberg article text, feed it to an AI model, and republish the output. Both licence their content, and both actively enforce. Summarising an article closely enough to be useful is a derivative work; doing it at scale, on a schedule, for a public site is the thing publishers litigate over.

What you can do:

  • Report facts in your own words. Facts are not copyrightable. "The Bank of England cut Bank Rate to 4.00% on a 6–3 vote" is a fact, and you can say it however you like.
  • Attribute and link. Name the outlet, link the original, never reproduce more than a short quotation.
  • Use primary sources directly. Central bank statements, statistics agencies, company results and IMF releases are free to use, publish on a schedule, and are what the FT is reporting on anyway.
  • Licence a feed if you go commercial. Aggregators sell redistribution rights. Read the terms on store-local versus redistribute — they differ, and the cheap tiers usually forbid the second.

The design implication

Build Ridar's summaries from primary sources plus multi-outlet corroboration, not from single articles. Instead of "summarise this FT piece", the pipeline asks: here are five headlines from five outlets, a central bank statement and today's market moves — write what happened and why it matters. That is defensible, better journalism, and produces the level-tiered explanation you actually want.

Table 1 — Start at tiers 1 and 2. They are enough for a working product.

Two practical notes. Aggregator free tiers usually cap you at a few hundred requests a day and delay results by 15–60 minutes — fine for a daily briefing, useless for trading. And headline plus URL is enough: you do not need full article text if your prompt is grounded in primary sources, which also keeps you clear of the licensing question.

Section 02Architecture

Figure 1 — One scheduled job a day; everything else is a read.

Nothing here needs to be real-time. A daily rebuild at a fixed hour is the right cadence for a learning product: it makes the content finite, which is what makes the "read today's briefing, take today's quiz" habit work.

Section 03Data schema

Eight tables. The shape follows the prototype's objects closely, so the front end changes very little.

-- reference
countries(iso3 pk, name, label, region, enabled bool)
topics(slug pk, label, sub, country_iso3s text[])

-- daily content
briefings(id pk, iso3 fk, date, sub, published_at)
stories(id pk, briefing_id fk, topic_slug fk null, headline,
        body_beginner, body_intermediate, body_advanced,
        sources jsonb,        -- [{outlet, url, published_at}]
        term_keys text[],     -- glossary refs used in the body
        rank int)
briefs(briefing_id fk, kind,  -- 'politics' | 'topic_what' | 'topic_ask'
       beginner, intermediate, advanced)
indicators(briefing_id fk, key, value, delta, direction)  -- direction: up|down|flat
events(briefing_id fk, date_label, text, sort int)        -- timeline
terms(key pk, term, beginner, intermediate, advanced)     -- glossary

-- quiz
questions(id pk, date, level, tag, prompt, options jsonb,
          correct_index int, explanation, iso3 fk null, topic_slug fk null)

-- users (Supabase auth handles credentials)
profiles(user_id pk, name, level, streak int, last_quiz_date)
attempts(id pk, user_id fk, date, level, score, total)
saved(user_id fk, iso3 fk, saved_at)
reads(user_id fk, iso3 fk, read_at)

Figure 2 — Schema sketch. Prototype constants map one-to-one onto these tables.

Two decisions worth making early. Keep the three level variants as columns on one row, not three rows: they describe the same story and must stay in sync. And store sources as JSON on the story rather than a join table — you only ever read them together, and it keeps attribution attached to the text it justifies.

Section 04The AI prompts

Three prompts. All three should request JSON output and be validated before anything is written. The critical instruction in each is the same: use only the supplied sources, and refuse rather than invent.

Prompt 1 — the country briefing

SYSTEM
You write commercial awareness briefings for finance students. You explain
what happened and why it matters. You do not give investment advice, make
predictions, or express political opinions.

RULES
1. Use ONLY the facts in the SOURCES block. If a number, date, vote count
   or name is not there, do not state it.
2. Never reproduce source wording. Write every sentence yourself.
3. If the sources are too thin for a story, return fewer stories. Never pad.
4. Write three versions of every text field:
   - beginner: no jargon at all. Any unavoidable term is defined in the
     sentence that uses it. Short sentences. Assume no prior knowledge.
   - intermediate: standard financial vocabulary used correctly, with
     mechanisms named (yield curve, term premium, guidance). Assume a
     second-year economics student.
   - advanced: second-order reasoning. What is the transmission channel,
     what would falsify the reading, what is the market actually pricing.
     Assume a candidate at final-round interview.
5. Mark jargon in the intermediate and advanced text with
   <span class="term" data-t="KEY">…</span> using keys from GLOSSARY.
6. No hype, no "this could mean", no emoji, no exclamation marks.

INPUT
COUNTRY: {name}
DATE: {date}
MARKET DATA: {indices, FX, policy rate, with deltas}
SOURCES: [{outlet, headline, url, published_at, summary_or_release_text}, …]
GLOSSARY KEYS: {available term keys}

OUTPUT (JSON)
{
  "sub": "one line, max 60 chars, what today is about",
  "stories": [{
    "headline": "...",
    "body_beginner": "...", "body_intermediate": "...", "body_advanced": "...",
    "term_keys": ["inflation"],
    "sources": [{"outlet":"...","url":"...","published_at":"..."}]
  }],
  "politics": {"beginner":"...","intermediate":"...","advanced":"..."},
  "timeline": [{"date_label":"12 Aug","text":"..."}]
}

Figure 3 — Run once per country per day. Two to three stories is the right length.

Prompt 2 — the quiz

SYSTEM
You write multiple-choice questions that test whether someone understood
today's briefings.

RULES
1. Every question must be answerable from the briefings supplied. No
   outside knowledge, no arithmetic on unstated numbers.
2. Test the mechanism, not recall of a number. "Why did the share price
   fall despite the earnings beat?" not "What was revenue?"
3. Four options. Exactly one correct. Wrong options must be plausible and
   wrong for a reason — the common misconception, not a joke answer.
4. The explanation teaches: say why the right answer is right AND why the
   tempting wrong one is wrong. Two or three sentences.
5. Difficulty by level:
   - beginner: definitions and direction of effect
   - intermediate: mechanism and second-order effect
   - advanced: what the market is pricing, what would falsify the reading
6. Vary which country and theme each question comes from.

INPUT
LEVEL: {beginner|intermediate|advanced}
BRIEFINGS: {today's stories for all enabled countries}

OUTPUT (JSON)
{"questions": [{
  "tag": "United Kingdom · Fixed income",
  "prompt": "...",
  "options": ["...","...","...","..."],
  "correct_index": 0,
  "explanation": "..."
}]}   // 8 per level; the app serves a deterministic daily 5

Figure 4 — Generate eight and serve five, so a repeat visit is not the same set.

Option order

Have the model always put the correct answer first and set correct_index: 0, then shuffle at render time with a daily seed — which is what the prototype now does. Asking a model to distribute correct answers randomly across positions produces a detectable bias; shuffling in code does not.

Prompt 3 — the daily digest

SYSTEM
You write the one-screen summary of the day's global picture, for someone
who has two minutes.

RULES
1. Use only the briefings supplied.
2. One paragraph naming the single thing that mattered most today and why.
3. Then three to four one-line items, each tied to a country, in the same
   three levels.
4. Say what connects them if anything does. If nothing does, say the day
   was quiet — do not manufacture a narrative.

OUTPUT (JSON)
{"headline":"...",
 "lede":{"beginner":"...","intermediate":"...","advanced":"..."},
 "items":[{"iso3":"GBR","text":{"beginner":"...","intermediate":"...","advanced":"..."}}],
 "thread":{"beginner":"...","intermediate":"...","advanced":"..."}}

Validation before writing

  • Reject any story whose sources array is empty.
  • Reject any question whose options length is not four, or whose correct_index is out of range.
  • Flag for review any number in the output that does not appear in the input sources — a simple numeric-token diff catches most hallucination.
  • Keep yesterday's briefing live until today's passes validation. Never publish a partial day.
  • Spot-check one country a day by hand for the first month. You will learn more about the prompts in that month than from any amount of tuning up front.

Section 05What the front end needs

The prototype's constants become fetches. Nothing else in the rendering logic has to change.

Prototype constantBecomes
COUNTRIESGET /api/briefings?date=today
TOPICSGET /api/topics?date=today
GLOSSARYGET /api/terms — cache indefinitely, it barely changes
QUESTIONSGET /api/quiz?level=i&date=today
DIGESTGET /api/digest?date=today
users / storeSupabase auth plus profiles, attempts, saved, reads. The prototype namespaces every storage key by user, so swapping the getters for API calls is a contained change.

Table 3 — Keying on ISO3 rather than country name is worth doing at the same time.

One thing to fix in the move: the prototype matches countries by the TopoJSON properties.name string, which means "United States of America" and not "United States". Map ISO3 codes to feature ids once, in a lookup, and the fragility goes away.

Two things to put on the site before it goes public

An explanation of how the summaries are made — that they are AI-written from named sources, and that readers should follow the links for the reporting itself. Attribution is both the legal position and the honest one.

A line saying this is education, not investment advice. Short, in the footer, and meant.
